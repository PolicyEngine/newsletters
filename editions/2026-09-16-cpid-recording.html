The new child poverty numbers, and how state reforms would affect them

Thank you for joining on Tuesday, or for registering if you could not make it. The recording, the slides, and the tools we showed are below.

Watch the recording

What we covered

The 2025 numbers, published that morning. Official child poverty fell 1.0 point to 13.4%, a record low. SPM child poverty stayed at 13.4%. Holding the SPM thresholds at their 2024 level plus inflation, child poverty fell 1.2 points; thresholds rising faster than prices, 6.3% for renters, added it back. Slides, with a state-by-state map of child poverty on slide 16.

Guest reactions. Cynthia Osborne (Prenatal-to-3 Policy Impact Center, Vanderbilt) on what states did for young children in 2025 and what to watch in 2026; Joshua McCabe (Niskanen Center) on housing costs and the SPM.

The Child Poverty Impact Dashboard, live. David Trimmer built a New York package on screen: a larger state child credit, a higher EITC match, the dependent exemption swapped for children, and two federal proposals. Pick a state, configure state and federal reforms, and see the effect on child poverty, budgets, income deciles, and congressional districts. Open the dashboard.

SPM thresholds, explained. How they are set, why they moved this year, and a calculator for any family and area, with the methods paper. SPM threshold calculator.

New from our guests. Cynthia Osborne's team has just released the 2026 Prenatal-to-3 State Policy Roadmap, which she previewed on the call: what every state did over the past year on evidence-based policies for infants and toddlers, including three new paid family and medical leave programs, a refundable EITC in Pennsylvania, and minimum wage increases in 23 states. Joshua McCabe's Family benefits in America 2025 report card shows how TANF, SNAP, and federal and state EITCs and CTCs add up for families with young children in each state, and the implicit marginal tax rates as parents move from no earnings to minimum-wage and median-wage work. Its TANF figures come from PolicyEngine.
