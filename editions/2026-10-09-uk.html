Three new UK dashboards, a UK marriage calculator and our AI incidence study

This edition: dashboards on the Resolution Foundation’s targeted energy bill discount, the Middle East war’s effect on UK living standards, and two free childcare reforms. Plus: our marriage calculator now covers the UK, and a study of who bears the cost if AI displaces UK jobs.

New Dashboard · Energy

Targeted energy bill discount

The Resolution Foundation proposes a winter energy bill discount for Great Britain: £175 for each household where someone receives a means-tested benefit or where no one has taxable income of £24,000 or more. Our dashboard, built with María Juaristi, models this flat design and four alternatives for 2026-27 and 2027-28.

What we find

• The flat £175 discount costs £2.09bn in 2026-27 and reaches 11.95m households, 41.8% of GB households, if every eligible household receives it.
• Absolute poverty before housing costs falls by about 140,000 people (0.21pp); this estimate rests on an effective sample of 19 households.
• People in the poorest tenth gain £153 a year on average, 0.84% of net income; the richest tenth gain £6. The model does not include how the discount is funded.

New Dashboard · Cost of living

Impact of the Middle East war on UK living standards

Energy, fuel and food prices rose after the Middle East conflict disrupted shipping through the Strait of Hormuz. Our dashboard runs three price scenarios through household budgets in 2027-28 as stress tests, not forecasts, and compares ten policy responses.

What we find

• At summer 2026 prices (energy +15%, fuel +22%, food +2%), households pay £538 more on average in 2027-28, £17.0bn in total. Under sustained escalation the figure rises to £1,334.
• The poorest fifth lose 1.65% of income at summer 2026 prices, against 0.60% for the richest fifth.
• Against a poverty line held at its pre-shock level (before housing costs), 379,000 more people fall into poverty, rising to 1.5m under sustained escalation.
• Of the ten responses, a flat £400 rebate costs £12.6bn with 43% reaching the bottom 40%; a social tariff costs £0.71bn with 86% reaching the bottom 40%.

New Dashboard · Childcare

Free childcare hours and a 75% subsidy

We cost two childcare reforms: 15 free hours a week for all children in England from 9 months, plus 15 more where parents work and earn under £100,000; and a UK-wide 75% subsidy replacing Tax-Free Childcare, with Universal Credit’s 85% childcare element kept.

What we find

• In 2027-28 the free hours cost £1.97bn and the subsidy £4.84bn before behavioural responses; with labour supply responses the costs fall to £1.93bn and £4.17bn.
• Working parents’ extra hours add up to 30,565 full-time equivalents across both reforms.
• Households with a child under 5 gain £2,099 a year on average. Gains rise with income, from £1,104 in the bottom fifth to £3,973 in the top, because Universal Credit already covers 85% of childcare costs for many lower-income families.

New · Research

Who bears the AI shock?

Our study traces AI employment, wage and capital shocks through the UK tax-benefit system with PolicyEngine UK and Family Resources Survey 2024-25 data. It runs scenarios, not predictions, across five assumptions about which workers bear the shock.

What we find

• In the central scenario, 7% of employees lose their jobs for the 2026 tax year and remaining workers get a 2.6% average wage rise. The Exchequer cost is £18.2bn, absolute poverty after housing costs rises by 1.85pp, and the Gini coefficient rises by 1.04pp.
• The tax-benefit system absorbs about a third of the loss in the top tenth: a market-income loss of 6.7% of disposable income becomes a 4.3% disposable-income loss.
• Delivering the same earnings loss through wage cuts instead of job losses costs the Exchequer more (£26.6bn) but raises poverty by only 0.10pp, and inequality falls.
