Peer reviewed · Journal of Open Source Software

policyengine: a microsimulation tool for tax-benefit policy analysis

The Journal of Open Source Software published our paper on the policyengine Python package on 14 September, after peer review. The package gives analysts one workflow for UK and US tax-benefit analysis: run current law or a reform on representative microdata or a custom household, with versioned release bundles so others can reproduce a published estimate. It also powers policyengine.org and every dashboard in this edition.

Ahmadi, Ghenis, Woodruff, Makarchuk and Volk (2026). Journal of Open Source Software, 11(125), 11115. doi:10.21105/joss.11115

01 · New dashboard · Energy

Targeted energy bill discount

The Resolution Foundation proposes a £175 winter energy bill discount in Great Britain for households where someone receives a means-tested benefit or where no one has taxable income of £24,000 or more. Our dashboard, built with María Juaristi, models this flat design and four alternatives for 2026-27 and 2027-28.

£2.09bn

cost in 2026-27 if every eligible household receives it

 

11.95m

households receive £175, 41.8% of GB households

 

~140k

fewer people in absolute poverty before housing costs
—The poverty estimate rests on an effective sample of 19 households, so we round it.
—People in the poorest tenth gain £153 a year on average (0.84% of net income); the richest tenth gain £6. The model does not include how the discount is funded.

02 · New dashboard · Cost of living

The Middle East war and UK living standards

Energy, fuel and food prices rose after the Middle East conflict disrupted shipping through the Strait of Hormuz. Our dashboard runs three price scenarios through household budgets in 2027-28 as stress tests, not forecasts, and compares ten policy responses.

£538

average extra cost per household at summer 2026 prices (£17.0bn in total)

 

1.65%

of income lost by the poorest fifth, against 0.60% for the richest

 

379k

more people would fall below a poverty line held at its pre-shock level
—The summer 2026 scenario raises energy spending by 15%, fuel by 22% and food by 2%.
—Under sustained escalation, households would pay £1,334 more on average and 1.5m more people would fall into poverty.
—The ten responses range in cost from £0.66bn (accelerated benefit uprating) to £12.6bn (a flat £400 rebate); the dashboard compares each one’s cost and the share reaching the poorest 40%.

03 · New dashboard · Childcare

Free childcare hours and a 75% subsidy

We cost two childcare reforms: 15 free hours a week for all children in England from 9 months, plus 15 more where parents work and earn under £100,000; and a UK-wide 75% subsidy replacing Tax-Free Childcare, keeping Universal Credit’s 85% childcare element.

£1.97bn

free hours, 2027-28, before behavioural responses

 

£4.84bn

75% subsidy, 2027-28, before behavioural responses

 

£2,099

average yearly gain for households with a child under 5
—With labour supply responses, the costs fall to £1.93bn and £4.17bn; under our assumptions, parents’ extra hours add up to an estimated 31,000 full-time equivalents.
—Gains rise with income, from £1,104 in the bottom fifth to £3,973 in the top, because Universal Credit already covers 85% of childcare costs for many lower-income families.

05 · Research · AI

Who bears the AI shock?

Our study traces AI employment, wage and capital shocks through the UK tax-benefit system with PolicyEngine UK and Family Resources Survey 2024-25 data. It runs scenarios, not predictions, across five assumptions about which workers bear the shock. In the central scenario, 7% of employees lose their jobs for the 2026 tax year and remaining workers get a 2.6% average wage rise.

£18.2bn

Exchequer cost in the central scenario

 

+1.85pp

absolute poverty after housing costs

 

+1.04pp

Gini coefficient
—The tax-benefit system would absorb about a third of the loss in the top tenth: a market-income loss of 6.7% of disposable income becomes a 4.3% disposable-income loss.
—Delivering the same earnings loss through wage cuts instead of job losses would cost the Exchequer more (£26.6bn) but raise absolute poverty by 0.10pp, and the Gini coefficient would fall.
