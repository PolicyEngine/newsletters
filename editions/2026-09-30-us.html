2025 Supplemental Poverty Measure

Our 2025 poverty prediction, graded

On September 11, four days before the Census Bureau’s release, we registered a prediction of 2025 Supplemental Poverty Measure rates. For all people we came within 0.1 point: 13.2 percent against Census’s 13.1. We missed by 0.9 points for children and 0.8 points for people 65 and over, in opposite directions, so the all-people result owed something to errors that offset.

We traced most of the senior miss, and part of the child miss, to how the model carries 2024 incomes into 2025. It grew each person’s Social Security, pensions and self-employment income at the rate of a national total, not at the rate recipients saw in the survey. Rerunning 2025 with rates closer to the survey’s removes most of the miss for people 65 and over and about a third of the miss for children.

What Census reported

SPM thresholds rose faster than prices

Census put SPM poverty at 13.1 percent in 2025, up 0.1 point, a change it does not count as statistically significant. The rate was 13.4 percent for children and 15.4 percent for people 65 and over. The official poverty rate fell 0.5 points to 10.2 percent. The SPM thresholds rose 4.4 to 6.3 percent depending on housing tenure, against 2.6 percent for consumer prices. At 2024 thresholds grown with inflation, the 2025 SPM rate would have been 12.3 percent, down 0.7 points.

Read the analysis, with the rates by age, housing tenure and state, or compute the threshold for any family and area with the SPM threshold calculator.

In the model

The model carries USDA’s fiscal year 2027 SNAP allotments, deductions and asset limits, which take effect in October. It extends child care assistance to children under court supervision, following each state’s age rules, in 34 states and DC. It now includes California’s County Children’s Health Initiative Program for children in San Francisco, San Mateo and Santa Clara counties.

We also corrected how SNAP treats child support paid, which the model had wrong for 46 jurisdictions in 2026, and counted IRA, 401(k) and other retirement plan distributions in state retirement income exclusions in 20 states, where state law allows. That is 81 policyengine-us releases since our September 8 newsletter.
