New Dashboard · Energy bills

Energy bills and the Autumn Budget 2026

Our new dashboard estimates what UK households will pay for gas and electricity in 2026-27 and 2027-28, and what seven Autumn Budget support options for 2027-28 would cost and who they would reach.

What we find

•In 2027-28, households would pay £386 more a year on average than at April–June 2026 prices.
•In 2027-28, a £400 payment to every household would cost £11.8bn, freezing the price cap at April–June 2026 tariffs £11.4bn and a £300 Cost of Living Payment on means-tested benefits £2.4bn.

New Dashboard · Energy

Targeted energy bill discount

The Resolution Foundation has proposed a discount that would cut winter energy bills by £175 on average for households in Great Britain on a qualifying means-tested benefit or where everyone’s annual taxable income is under £24,000. Our new dashboard models it as a flat £175 for each eligible household and estimates its cost and reach, alongside four other designs.

What we find

•At full take-up, the flat £175 payment would cost £2.09bn in 2026-27 and reach 11.95m households.
•With people ranked by equivalised household income after housing costs, households in the bottom decile gain £153 on average in 2026-27 and those in the top decile £6.

New Dashboard · Childcare

Free childcare hours and a 75% subsidy

Explore the cost and household impact of two childcare reforms. The first gives every child in England from 9 months to school age 15 funded hours a week for 38 weeks a year, or 30 where parents work and each earns under £100,000. The second replaces Tax-Free Childcare with a UK-wide 75% subsidy and keeps Universal Credit childcare support. The free-hours costing leaves out children aged 9 to 11 months because the survey records age in whole years.

What we find

•Costed separately and including parents’ labour supply responses, the free hours cost £1.93bn and the subsidy £4.17bn in 2027-28.
•With both reforms together and including labour supply responses, households with a child under 5 gain £2,099 a year on average in 2027-28.

New Research · AI

Who bears the AI shock?

If AI displaces a share of UK jobs, who bears the loss: workers, lower-income households or the public finances? Our new study runs AI-driven job losses, wage changes and higher returns to capital through the UK tax-benefit system. It compares five assumptions about which workers AI affects.

What we find

•Our central scenario has 7% of employees losing their jobs for the whole 2026 tax year, a 2.6% average pay rise for those who stay and higher returns to capital. It would cost the Exchequer £18.2bn and raise absolute poverty after housing costs by 1.85pp.
•Delivering the same gross earnings loss through wage cuts instead would cost £26.6bn but raise absolute poverty after housing costs by 0.27pp.
