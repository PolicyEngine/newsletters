Why the Prop 40 revenue estimates differ

Proposition 40, the 2026 Billionaire Tax Act on California’s November 3 ballot, would impose a one-time tax on the net worth, as of December 31, 2026, of people who lived in California on January 1. The rate rises from zero at $1 billion to 5 percent at $1.1 billion, and the tax excludes directly held real property. Published estimates run from about $100 billion in revenue to a net loss in present value once they count the California income tax the state would lose as billionaires leave.

Max Ghenis and David Trimmer walk through PolicyEngine’s open calculator for the measure. It scores Prop 40 person by person from current Forbes data, applies the Berkeley and Hoover assumptions inside one model, and splits the gap between them by assumption. Fernando Hoces de la Guardia then presents his Open Policy Analysis reconciliation of the two analyses, and the three of them take your questions. We’ll post the recording.

What the calculator finds

On Forbes data as of October 29:

• With no behavioral response, the tax collects $117.6 billion of one-time revenue.
• Inside one model, the Berkeley assumptions give a net present value of $103.1 billion; the Hoover assumptions, which follow Jaros and Rauh’s NBER version, give −$34.1 billion.
• Three assumptions account for most of that gap: counting movers’ future income tax (−$66.4 billion), the chance that legal challenges stop collection (−$53.7 billion) and documented departures (−$23.2 billion).
• Our working paper finds that the two teams’ estimates of the billionaires’ total California income tax overlap; they disagree on who pays it. On the October 23 snapshot, dividing $4.55 billion by wealth assigns the four largest fortunes $1,785 million a year, where estimates from their securities filings put them at $252 million.

Read the working paper, or open the calculator, which refreshes its Forbes data daily.

2025 Supplemental Poverty Measure

How our 2025 poverty prediction scored

On September 11, four days before the Census Bureau’s release, we registered a prediction of 2025 SPM poverty rates. For all people we came within 0.1 point: 13.2 percent against Census’s 13.1. We missed by 0.9 points for children and 0.8 points for people 65 and over, in opposite directions. In carrying 2024 incomes into 2025, the model grew Social Security, pensions and self-employment income at the rate of a national total, not at the rate recipients in the survey saw; growing them at rates closer to the survey’s cuts the child miss by about a third and the senior miss from 0.8 points to between 0.1 and 0.3. Read the grade.

Census put SPM poverty at 13.1 percent, up 0.1 point, while the SPM thresholds rose 4.4 to 6.3 percent against 2.6 percent for consumer prices; our analysis breaks the rates down by age, housing tenure and state. PolicyBench

GPT-6 Sol takes the lead

PolicyBench, our open benchmark with the Axiom Foundation, tests how accurately language models calculate household taxes and benefits. On the October 14 board of 50 models, GPT-6 Sol leads with an exact-match score of 95.2 percent, where dollar answers within $1 count as exact, and every model in the top ten scores above 90 percent. A September audit of the answer key against the law found 20 defects in the policyengine-us version behind it.

Max gave a lightning talk on how accurately AI calculates benefits at Nava’s Open Source Summit: AI Futures in Baltimore on October 14. On October 26 in Phoenix, he and Ariel Kennan of the Axiom Foundation ran an evening session for the Aspen Institute’s State Benefits Leadership Cohort: state benefits executives put real benefits questions to a chatbot, rated its answers, then saw on PolicyBench how the models hold up across 100 households.

Journal of Open Source Software

A peer-reviewed paper on our Python package

The Journal of Open Source Software published our peer-reviewed paper on policyengine, the open-source Python package behind PolicyEngine. The package gives one workflow for the US and the UK: calculate taxes and benefits for a single household, or simulate the whole population on representative survey data, and compare current law with a reform. Vahid Ahmadi, Max Ghenis, Nikhil Woodruff, Pavel Makarchuk and Anthony Volk wrote it. If you use PolicyEngine in your work, please cite the paper.

In the model

Since September 8, we added regular heating assistance from LIHEAP and state energy programs in 26 states, two of them (Ohio and Nebraska) in part, and state supplements to SSI in 17 more states. The model now also computes payments under the federal student loan repayment plans, from Income-Based Repayment to the Repayment Assistance Plan that took effect on July 1, as well as New York City’s income tax elimination credit and 12-month Medicaid postpartum coverage in the 50 jurisdictions that elected it.

It now uses USDA’s fiscal year 2027 SNAP allotments, deductions and asset limits, and the 2027 Social Security and SSI cost-of-living parameters. We also corrected how SNAP treats child support paid, which the model had wrong for 46 jurisdictions in 2026, and counted IRA, 401(k) and other retirement plan distributions in state retirement income exclusions in 20 states, where state law allows. These changes span more than 260 policyengine-us releases since our September 8 newsletter.
